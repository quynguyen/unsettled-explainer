Explainer · Climate science, models & uncertainty

Unsettled, explained

Steven E. Koonin's book about what climate science does and doesn't tell us. This page covers its strongest ideas, its figures and how much weight each can bear, the best case its critics make, and a closer look at climate science as climate modelling.

TL;DR

  • The book: Unsettled (BenBella, 2021; updated and expanded paperback June 2024) by physicist Steven Koonin, a former BP chief scientist and Obama-era DOE Under Secretary for Science.1,5 He accepts that the world is warming and that humans are contributing. What he disputes is the certainty, the size of the impacts and the urgency that reach the public.
  • Core move: he reads the body of the IPCC and US assessment reports against their summaries and the headlines. He finds that many confident public claims, about hurricanes, US heat waves, sea-level acceleration and economic ruin, have low-confidence or no-trend findings underneath them in the reports themselves.
  • Most insightful bits: human forcing is a ~1% nudge on enormous energy flows. Models are tuned, are coarse, and disagree with each other by ~3 °C on the planet's absolute temperature. The sensitivity range stayed at 1.5–4.5 °C from 1979 until 2021. And a projected 3% GDP loss by 2100 works out to about two years of lost growth.
  • Best critique: he picks the record that favours his point (e.g. one sea-level dataset, burned area including farm fires). He ignores the physics-based projections that matter for the future. And he treats uncertainty as reassuring, when a wide range with a fat tail means more risk. Several of his points have also been overtaken since 2021: AR6 narrowed sensitivity to 2.5–4 °C and detected more extreme-event trends.
  • Modelling verdict: climate models have a real track record on global temperature (14 of 17 past projections were consistent with observations once forcing is accounted for).12 They are weak on regional rainfall and on timing, and poor at linking specific events to climate change. Even so, they beat macro-economic forecasting, which missed 148 of 153 recessions a year ahead.30 The models are anchored in conservation laws, and the climate doesn't react to forecasts about it the way an economy does.

1What the book is about

Unsettled: What Climate Science Tells Us, What It Doesn't, and Why It Matters is a book for general readers. It examines the gap between climate research and the climate story that reaches the public. The publisher's description puts the problem as "the long game of telephone from research to reports to the popular media," which it calls "corrupted by misunderstanding and misinformation."2

It roughly splits in two. The science half covers the energy balance, what models can and can't do, temperature records, heat waves, hurricanes, sea level, and other extremes. The response half covers economic impacts, why deep decarbonisation is hard, and the case for adaptation, plus geoengineering "if necessary."2 The June 2024 updated and expanded edition adds a new introduction. It also covers three more years of data, alternatives to "net zero," global energy inequality, and the energy crisis that followed the war in Ukraine.1

What Koonin does not claimHe doesn't deny warming or human influence. In his 2014 WSJ essay he wrote that "there is little doubt in the scientific community that continually growing amounts of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, due largely to carbon-dioxide emissions from the conventional use of fossil fuels, are influencing the climate."3 His argument is about how much, how fast, how well we can know it, and what to do about it.

Who is Koonin?

Career

He was a Caltech theoretical physics professor for almost three decades, and Caltech's Provost for nine of those years. He was BP's Chief Scientist from 2004 and US DOE Under Secretary for Science from May 2009 to November 2011. From 2012 he was at NYU (Stern / Center for Urban Science and Progress).5

The road to the book

In January 2014 he chaired an American Physical Society workshop to review the APS climate statement. Its panel included three mainstream scientists and three well-known dissenters: Judith Curry, Richard Lindzen and John Christy.4,7 Eight months later he published "Climate Science Is Not Settled" in the Wall Street Journal (Sept 19, 2014).3 The book grew out of that essay.

2Why it was written

Stated motive: informed choices need honest uncertainty. In 2014 Koonin framed the policy question as more than "is it changing?" or "are humans influencing it?":

"Rather, the crucial, unsettled scientific question for policy is, 'How will the climate change over the next century under both natural and human influences?'"Koonin, WSJ, Sept 19, 20143

He thinks the slogan "the science is settled" shuts down exactly that discussion. In the book he separates the research literature, which is full of error bars, caveats and low-confidence findings, from the simplified public version he calls "The Science" (capitalised). That version is a fixed message about catastrophe and urgency that gets attached to the authority of science.

Diagram: the "telephone game" from research to headline

A diagram of Koonin's argument, not data. SPMs really are approved line by line in plenary sessions with government delegates. The authors sign off that the text matches the underlying report, but the wording is negotiated.

Summaries vs. body. Koonin's main method is to set statements from the Summary for Policymakers or the US National Climate Assessment beside the more cautious tables and chapter text underneath. One example from his economic chapter: the 2018 National Climate Assessment's key message warned of losses of "hundreds of billions of dollars" in some sectors. Its own end-of-chapter figure showed a US GDP impact of about 4% at a very large +5 °C warming by 2090.5

Concrete example: the hurricane caseAR5 (2013) said there was low confidence in long-term (centennial) changes in tropical cyclone activity. The 2017 US Climate Science Special Report highlighted an upward trend in North Atlantic activity since the 1970s. Koonin's point is that the start date matters: the 1970s were a quiet period, and longer records don't show a clear trend. His critics answer that the newer post-1970s satellite-era record is the reliable one, and that intensity rather than frequency is where the signal shows up.6,9,11

Personal motive. Koonin says speaking openly about uncertainty felt like breaking a "code of silence" in the field. Boslough quotes him comparing it to the Mafia's omertà, and criticises that language as biased.7 Koonin's defenders say the hostile response to the book only proved his point.42

4The subtle, genuinely insightful ideas

Whatever you think of his conclusions, several of Koonin's points are true and under-appreciated. Many critics concede them (the American Journal of Physics review: "On these points, and others like them, Koonin is largely correct").6 These are worth keeping even if you reject the book's overall message.

4.1 Detection vs. attribution: two different questions

Detection asks whether a change is statistically distinguishable from natural variability. Attribution asks how much of a detected change is caused by humans. You can't attribute what you haven't detected, but headlines often jump straight to attribution. For noisy, rare events like hurricanes, floods and droughts, the signal-to-noise ratio is low. Detection can take decades of good data, and good data often starts only in the satellite era (~1979).

Concrete exampleA coin that lands heads 55% of the time looks fair after 20 flips (11 heads is ordinary). To detect the bias at 95% confidence you need roughly 400 flips. Category 4–5 Atlantic landfalls happen a few times a decade, so a real change in their rate can exist for a long time before it can be detected. "No detected trend" and "no effect" are not the same statement. Critics use exactly this point against Koonin.

4.2 A 1% perturbation in a chaotic system

Absorbed sunlight (~240 W/m²) and emitted heat are almost perfectly balanced. The human nudge (~2.7 W/m²) is a little over 1% of absorbed sunlight, and the current imbalance it produces is under 1 W/m².15 To model the response you have to get the difference between two large numbers right. Clouds alone reflect tens of W/m², so a small fractional error in cloud cover is as big as the whole human signal. This is the most physically interesting idea in the book.

4.3 Grid cells and parameterisation

Global models divide the planet into cells typically ~100 km or more across. Clouds, thunderstorms and turbulence happen on scales from metres to a few km, far below a cell. The models can't simulate them. They parameterise them with simplified rules that have adjustable coefficients. That's where tuning comes in, and it's why cloud feedback is the largest source of spread in climate sensitivity.6,15

4.4 Tuning, and why matching the 20th century proves less than it seems

Modellers openly adjust parameters, mainly to get the top-of-atmosphere energy balance right and sometimes with the 20th-century warming in view.22,23 If a model was tuned to the 20th century, matching the 20th century is a fit to data it has already seen, not a prediction of something new. Hourdin et al. (2017) urge modelling centres to document their tuning targets for exactly this reason.23 A sharper version of the point: models with quite different sensitivities can all match the historical record because their aerosol forcing differs. Aerosol cooling can cancel out some of the greenhouse warming.

4.5 "Ensembles of opportunity"

The CMIP ensemble isn't a designed random sample of possible models. It's whatever models the world's modelling centres chose to submit, and many of them share code and ancestry. Statisticians call this an ensemble of opportunity.24 The spread across the ensemble isn't a true probability distribution, and averaging models that share errors doesn't cancel those errors out. This is why AR6 stopped simply taking the CMIP6 average and built "assessed" warming from several lines of evidence instead.14

4.6 Forty-two years of the same sensitivity range

The Charney report (1979) said 3 °C ± 1.5 °C. AR5 (2013) said likely 1.5–4.5 °C and gave no best estimate.16,15 No other headline number in modern physics stayed this uncertain for that long. It shows the hard part (feedbacks, especially clouds) wasn't solved by more computing power. AR6 did finally narrow it, to likely 2.5–4 °C, but mostly by combining paleoclimate, historical observations and process studies, not by running better models (Fig. 5.3).14,15

4.7 Percent of GDP versus growth

A "3% of GDP loss by 2100" sounds large. But if the economy grows ~2%/yr, it will be several times bigger by then, and 3% is roughly a year or two of growth.5 Comparing the damage to the growth rate is a useful sanity check that headlines rarely do (Fig. 5.7). Its weakness is that the result is only as good as the damage function behind it, as §6 shows.

4.8 The language of assessments

The IPCC uses calibrated terms. Likely means ≥66% probability, very likely ≥90%, virtually certain ≥99%. Confidence (low, medium, high) is a separate scale for how strong the evidence and agreement are.17 The subtle point is that these words are precise inside the reports but ambiguous outside them. "Likely" reads as near-certain in a headline. "Low confidence in a trend" gets read as "no effect" by sceptics or ignored by advocates. And the SPM's choice of which findings to headline is itself a judgement call.

6Steelmanning the critics

This is the strongest fair version of the case against Unsettled, drawn from climate scientists' reviews. Insults and credential-policing are left out unless they bear on an argument.

1. Selective data: true facts, misleading pictureHausfather on the WSJ review of the book: "a mix of factually correct, correct but misleading, and inaccurate statements."10 His examples: citing one sea-level dataset and ignoring four that show clear acceleration; citing declining global burned area, which is driven by fewer deliberately set agricultural fires, while wildfires increase in many regions; and Greenland. NSIDC's Twila Moon notes average ice loss in 2003–2010 was about 2.5× the 1900–2003 rate.10 Every fact can be accurate and the selection still misleading.
2. "No detected trend" is not "no effect"Detection limits (§4.1) cut both ways. A low-confidence trend in noisy data is exactly what you'd expect early in a real change. The AJP reviewer, who agrees with Koonin on many observational points, faults him for ignoring physics-based projections. Those "tell us unanimously that heat waves will increase, sea level rise will accelerate, and hurricanes will intensify on a warmer planet."6 Physical reasoning (warmer air holds ~7% more water vapour per °C) is evidence too, not just trend lines.
3. Uncertainty is not reassuranceBoslough: Koonin seems "unaware of the fact that increased uncertainty means increased risks." Sensitivity is a right-skewed distribution "with a long high-sensitivity tail that we cannot discount with certainty."7 In risk management, expected loss is probability × consequence summed over outcomes. If damages grow faster than linearly with warming, a wider range raises the expected cost. Weitzman's "dismal theorem" argued that fat tails can dominate cost–benefit analysis.36
4. Models have a forecasting track recordKoonin stresses tuning and hindcasts. But models published before the warming happened projected it well: 10 of 17 projections from 1970–2007 matched raw observations, and 14 of 17 did after correcting for forcing that differed from what was assumed, with no systematic bias towards running hot or cold.12 You can't tune a 1988 model to 2017 data. That's an out-of-sample test.
5. The field already fixed the problems he highlightsThe "hot model" issue was recognised by modellers themselves (Hausfather, Marvel, Schmidt et al., Nature 2022). AR6 didn't use the raw CMIP6 mean. It assessed warming from multiple lines of evidence and narrowed ECS to 2.5–4 °C.14 AR6 also upgraded detection for heat, heavy rain and the share of major tropical cyclones.17 Yohe argues that uncertainty has "narrowed or become better understood" with each assessment.8
6. The economics leans on the weakest model of allKoonin is sceptical of climate models yet treats the AR5 economic damage estimates as reliable enough to call impacts "negligible." Dessler argues that climate-economics damage functions lack solid data or theory.37 Estimates since then run up to ~23% of income (Burke et al.).35 Global GDP averages also hide concentrated losses: even the 2018 review Koonin cites acknowledges "severe impacts on less developed countries."5 And GDP doesn't count lost ecosystems or lives well.
7. A strawman target and lopsided advisersBoslough argues that "the science is settled" is rarely said by climate scientists themselves. He also says the 2014 APS workshop's 3 mainstream vs 3 contrarian panel lacked proportionate representation and amplified contrary opinions, and that the book's errors trace back to trusting those advisers.7 Koonin is a distinguished computational physicist, but he hasn't published primary climate research. Critics see that as a reason to check his readings of the literature carefully, not as a disqualification in itself.

Koonin's replies

Koonin answered Scientific American on Medium (they declined to publish it). He says the critics responded to a newspaper column rather than the book. He says Chapter 6 reviews hurricane research through 2020, including a 2019 eleven-expert assessment, and none of it claims detectable human influence on hurricanes.11,41 And he says Chapter 8 doesn't portray sea level as "steady" but describes its decadal variability.11 He closes by paraphrasing Einstein: "If I were wrong, it wouldn't take a dozen scientists to disprove me — one would be sufficient."11 Defenders such as Reason argue that critics often conceded his facts while objecting to his framing.42

A fair scorecardOn narrow observational claims (US heat-wave index, hurricane frequency, flood trends, the stuck ECS range up to AR5, absolute-temperature bias), Koonin is mostly right, and mainstream reviewers say so.6 On the overall picture (that the risks are modest and urgency is overstated), he relies on selective records, discounts physics-based projections, and treats uncertainty as if it only cut one way.

7Climate science as climate modelling

The framing: much of "climate science" that reaches policy isn't like mathematics, where a proof is checkable, verifiable and reproducible forever. It's modelling, and a model is only as useful as its track record in predicting:

WHAT happens

Which variables change, and in which direction (warmer, wetter, drier, more ice or less).

HOW MUCH

The size of the change (degrees, millimetres, percent).

WHEN

The timing and rate, including thresholds and tipping points.

WHY

The causal story: which forcing produced which change.

This framing is largely right, and it's the most useful lens on the whole Koonin debate. The disputes are almost entirely about model skill on particular dimensions at particular scales. But it needs three refinements to be fully accurate:

Refinement 1: part of the foundation is lab-reproducible physicsCO₂ absorbs infrared radiation. Tyndall measured it in the lab in the 1850s, and the absorption lines are catalogued spectroscopy. The extra downward radiation from rising CO₂ has been directly measured at the surface: about 0.2 W/m² per decade at two US sites from 2000 to 2010 (Feldman et al. 2015).40 Conservation of energy, mass and momentum aren't up for debate either. The modelling uncertainty sits in the feedbacks (clouds, water vapour, ice) and in regional dynamics, not in the greenhouse effect itself.
Refinement 2: projections are conditional, not forecastsA climate projection says: if emissions follow scenario X, the climate responds like Y. Most uncertainty in 2100 temperature comes from which emissions path humanity chooses, not from the physics.14 That's why Hausfather et al. score old models on warming per unit of forcing.12 It's a fair test of the physics, and it separates out human choices the modellers couldn't know.
Refinement 3: skill depends on scale"Climate models" isn't one thing to grade. The same model can be excellent at the global annual mean and poor at July rainfall in Kenya. As the AJP reviewer puts it, "any model will be fit for some purposes and not for others."6 Overall pass/fail verdicts in either direction miss this.

7.2 Economic modelling's track record

RecessionsAn, Jalles & Loungani (IMF, 2018) studied GDP forecasts for 63 countries, 1992–2014. Forecasters missed 148 of 153 recessions as of April of the preceding year, and missed the magnitude "by a wide margin until the year is almost over." Private and official forecasts were "virtually identical; thus, both are equally good at missing recessions."30,31
The FedThe Federal Reserve's own benchmark of historical forecast errors puts the typical (RMS) error for real GDP growth at roughly 2 percentage points at longer horizons. That's about as large as normal growth itself. Unemployment errors also reach about 2 points.32 2008 is the canonical miss: few mainstream models gave a financial-crisis recession any meaningful probability beforehand.
StructureDSGE models, the mainstream macro workhorse, have been criticised from within the field. Paul Romer's "The Trouble with Macroeconomics" (2016) argued they rest on unverifiable identifying assumptions.39 The Lucas critique (1976) notes that relationships estimated from past data break down when policy changes, because people adapt.38

7.3 Why climate models have a structural advantage

Conservation laws

Energy, mass and momentum are conserved, and radiative transfer is known physics. That rules out most wrong answers before any tuning. Economics has accounting identities but no equivalent of the first law of thermodynamics for behaviour.

No reflexivity

CO₂ molecules don't read IPCC reports. Markets react to forecasts: a predicted recession can trigger one, and a predicted crash gets priced in. Economic forecasts change what they forecast, and climate forecasts don't. (Only the emissions scenario is human and reflexive.)

Large-number averaging

The global annual mean averages out a huge amount of chaotic weather, so the forced signal stands out. Recessions are rare, discrete turning points, and that's exactly what models handle worst.

Out-of-sample tests exist

Old climate projections can be checked against decades of later data (Hausfather 2020). Macro has many vintage forecasts too, and their scores are what make the record look bad (Loungani).
Where the comparison cuts against climateRegional rainfall, extreme-event frequency and tipping-point timing are about as hard to predict as recessions: rare, nonlinear, threshold-driven. And the policy question ultimately runs through integrated assessment models such as Nordhaus's DICE, which bolt an economic damage function onto a simple climate model. Their output (the social cost of carbon, "optimal" warming) inherits economics' uncertainty on top of the climate uncertainty. That's Koonin's "doubly dismal" point,5 and Dessler's too, from the other side.37 The retraction of a high-profile 2024 damage paper shows how fragile this layer is.33

7.4 Scorecard: climate vs economic modelling on the four questions EDITORIAL JUDGEMENT

0–5 bars represent this explainer's qualitative assessment of the evidence above, not a measured statistic. Climate = global-scale physical models. Economics = macro forecasting models. "Why" for economics reflects contested causal identification (Romer, Lucas).

7.5 Forecast horizon vs. skill ILLUSTRATIVE / CONCEPTUAL

The curve shapes are conceptual, not data. They encode documented facts: weather skill fades within roughly two weeks (chaos). Global-mean climate projections regain skill at multi-decade horizons because the forced signal outgrows the noise, as long as the scenario is right. Regional rainfall stays low. Macro forecasts have some skill a few quarters out but are close to no-skill for turning points a year ahead (Loungani).

7.6 Bottom line

Where the framing is rightClimate science that informs policy is mostly modelling, and it should be judged by its predictions on what, how much, when and why. By that standard, regional, extreme-event and timing predictions are genuinely weak, and the 34 years of unchanged ECS range was a fair embarrassment. Treating model outputs as "settled" facts with the authority of mathematics is a category error.
Where it oversimplifiesThe core (CO₂ forcing, energy conservation) is reproducible physics, not just modelling. Projections are conditional on emissions, so "the model was wrong" often means "the scenario differed." On the dimension that matters most for the global picture, how much the planet warms per unit of forcing, climate models have a better documented track record than almost any macro-economic model. And uncertainty means a wider range of outcomes, including worse ones.

8Glossary

Radiative forcing (ERF)
A change in Earth's energy balance (W/m²) caused by an external driver such as CO₂, aerosols or the sun. Humans: ~2.7 W/m² since 1750.
ECS: equilibrium climate sensitivity
The long-run warming after CO₂ doubles. AR6: likely 2.5–4 °C, best estimate 3 °C.
TCR: transient climate response
Warming at the moment CO₂ doubles, under a 1%/yr increase. More relevant to this century, and better constrained.
CMIP
The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, the coordinated set of model runs (CMIP5, CMIP6) that the IPCC draws on.
Parameterisation
A simplified rule standing in for processes smaller than a grid cell, such as clouds, convection and turbulence.
Tuning
Adjusting uncertain parameters so a model reproduces chosen observations, such as the energy balance or past climate.
Ensemble of opportunity
A collection of models gathered by availability rather than by design, so its spread isn't a true probability distribution.
Detection / attribution
Detection: showing a change is beyond natural variability. Attribution: estimating how much of it is due to a specific cause.
Anomaly
A departure from a baseline average. Models are usually compared as anomalies, which hides their absolute-temperature differences.
SPM
Summary for Policymakers, the short IPCC summary approved line by line with governments.
RCP / SSP scenarios
Assumed paths for emissions and socioeconomic development. RCP8.5 / SSP5-8.5 is a high-emissions case that critics on both sides now consider unlikely.
Integrated assessment model (IAM)
A model linking an economy, emissions, simple climate physics and a damage function, e.g. Nordhaus's DICE.
Damage function
The assumed relationship between warming and economic loss. It's the most uncertain part of an IAM.
DSGE
Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model, the standard central-bank macro model.
Lucas critique
Relationships estimated from past data change when policy changes, because people respond.
Reflexivity
When a forecast changes the outcome it forecasts. This is common in economics and absent in physical climate.
